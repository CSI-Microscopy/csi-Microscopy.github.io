Plain-language guide

Microscopy basics for new users

Seven things that decide whether your images are usable, explained without jargon. Read this before your first session. It takes about ten minutes.

1. Magnification is not detail 2. Pixels: how fine to sample 3. Exposure: do not saturate 4. Fluorescence channels 5. Going 3D: z-stacks 6. Crops, zooms and scale bars 7. Files, metadata and backup Glossary
1 · Resolution

Magnification is not the same as detail

Zooming in makes things bigger. It does not make them sharper. What decides sharpness is a number engraved on every objective: the numerical aperture, or NA.

Think of the objective as a funnel that collects light from your sample. A wide funnel (high NA, up to about 1.4 for oil objectives) catches light from steep angles and can tell two nearby points apart. A narrow funnel (low NA, around 0.3 for a 10× objective) blurs them into one.

Two objectives with the same magnification can have very different NA. The NA is the second number on the barrel, for example 40×/1.30. When you choose an objective, choose by NA, not by magnification.

The smallest distance an objective can still separate is called its resolution. A simple rule, due to Ernst Abbe, gives it as:

resolution ≈ wavelength ÷ (2 × NA) Green light of about 520 nm and an NA of 1.3 gives 520 ÷ 2.6 = 200 nm. Nothing smaller than that will be resolved, whatever the zoom.

Confocal microscopes get a little below this, and STED (on the Stellaris) gets well below it. Widefield systems such as the EVOS sit at this limit.

How the numbers are worked out
  • Lateral resolution = wavelength ÷ (2 × NA). Abbe's limit. Divide the wavelength by twice the NA. Wider light-collecting angle (bigger NA) or bluer light (smaller wavelength) gives a smaller, better number.
  • Axial resolution = 2 × wavelength × n ÷ NA². Depth resolution is always worse than lateral. It depends on the refractive index n of the immersion medium and on the square of the NA, so a high-NA objective improves depth resolution a lot.
  • Pixel size = lateral resolution ÷ 2.3. This is the Nyquist rule: to record a detail you need at least two pixels across it. We use 2.3 rather than 2 to leave a safety margin. Larger pixels lose detail the objective did deliver; much smaller pixels only add noise and file size.
  • Z-step = axial resolution ÷ 2.3. The same rule applied along the focus axis.
  • The calculator refuses an NA larger than the refractive index of the medium, because a dry objective physically cannot exceed NA 1.0, a water objective 1.33, and so on.

These are approximations for widefield and confocal imaging. They are good enough to choose an objective and set a pixel size. The code lives in basics.js, in the function named updateCalc.

2 · Sampling

Pixels: how finely to sample

A digital image is a grid of squares. Each square holds one brightness value. If the squares are larger than the details, the details are gone and no software can bring them back.

On a camera system (EVOS) the pixel size is fixed by the camera and the objective. On a scanning system (confocal, STED, light sheet) you choose it with the zoom and the number of pixels per line. The software usually offers an "optimal" or "Nyquist" button that sets it for you. Use it.

The rule: at least two pixels across the smallest detail you care about. The calculator above gives the number for your objective.

Oversampling (pixels much smaller than needed) is not free. On a confocal it means more laser exposure per area, slower scans and larger files. It does not add real detail.

In Fiji you can check the pixel size of any image under Image → Properties. If it says 1 pixel = 1 pixel, the calibration was lost somewhere, usually by saving as JPEG or PNG. See section 7.

3 · Exposure

Bright enough, but never saturated

Every pixel can store brightness only up to a maximum. Anything brighter is clipped to that maximum. Once clipped, the information is gone and the image cannot be used for measurements.

An image that looks good on screen can still be saturated. Screens show white for anything above a threshold, so you cannot see clipping by eye. Look at the histogram instead: it is a bar chart of how many pixels have each brightness.

  • Aim for the brightest structures to sit around 50 to 70 % of the maximum. That leaves room for brighter samples in the same experiment.
  • Use the saturation display: on the Leica systems it is the QLUT button; in Fiji it is the HiLo lookup table. Saturated pixels turn blue or red so you see them at once.
  • Set exposure or detector gain on your brightest sample, then keep it fixed for every sample you want to compare. Changing settings between samples makes the numbers incomparable.
  • Too dark is also bad: a dim image is mostly noise. Raise exposure time before raising gain, because gain amplifies noise along with signal.
4 · Fluorescence

Channels, dyes and bleed-through

A fluorescent dye absorbs light of one colour and gives back light of a slightly longer colour. The microscope separates the two, so you see only the dye against a dark background.

Each dye is recorded in its own channel: a combination of excitation light and a detector window. When you image several dyes, the channels must be far enough apart that one dye does not show up in the other's channel. When it does, the result is called bleed-through or crosstalk, and it produces false "co-localisation".

  • Choose dyes with well-separated emission colours. Three dyes spread over blue, green and far red are safer than three in green, yellow and orange.
  • Always image a sample stained with only one dye at a time (a single-stain control) with the full multi-colour settings. Any signal in the other channels is bleed-through.
  • On the confocal, record channels one after another (sequential mode) rather than simultaneously. It is slower and much cleaner.
  • Fluorescence fades with light exposure (photobleaching). Find your field of view with low light or transmitted light, then take the fluorescence image once.
  • Plant tissue glows by itself (autofluorescence), especially chlorophyll in the red and cell walls in the blue and green. Image an unstained sample to know what you are up against.

The Bitesize Bio article on choosing a fluorophore palette goes deeper, and the staff can help you pick a combination that works on our systems.

5 · 3D

Going 3D: z-stacks and voxels

A confocal, light-sheet or microCT image of a thick sample is a stack of slices taken at different depths. Each slice is a normal 2D image; together they form a 3D block of voxels, the 3D equivalent of pixels.

The distance between slices (the z-step) is a choice you make, exactly like the pixel size. Steps that are too large miss structures between slices, and the stack cannot be rotated or re-sliced without gaps.

Voxels are almost never cubes. A typical confocal stack might have 0.1 µm pixels in x and y but a 0.5 µm step in z. That is fine as long as the software knows it. Most analysis tools read the step from the file. If you convert formats and the step is lost, every 3D measurement will be wrong by that ratio.

Use the "optimal" z-step in the acquisition software, or the calculator above. On the microCT and light sheet the voxels are usually cubic by design.

The FocalPlane article on pixel anisotropy explains how to handle non-cubic voxels when you process the data.

6 · Presenting images

Crops, zooms and scale bars

Readers need two views: the whole field, so they know where they are, and a close-up of the detail that matters. A crop with its position marked on the overview gives them both. Every image needs a scale bar.

How to make a crop that is honest

  • Crop, do not resample. Cutting out a region keeps every pixel. Enlarging the crop afterwards with "smooth" interpolation invents pixels that were never recorded. If you must enlarge for print, say so, and use nearest-neighbour so pixels stay visible.
  • Show where the crop came from. Draw its outline on the overview, as above. In Fiji: draw a rectangle, then Edit → Selection → Add to Manager keeps it; Image → Crop cuts it out; Image → Duplicate first so you keep the original.
  • Same settings for every panel you compare. Brightness and contrast must be identical across panels that are meant to be compared. Changing them per panel is a common way to mislead, even by accident.
  • Keep the original. Crops and adjusted copies are for the figure. The analysis runs on the untouched file.

Scale bars

  • A scale bar is a line of known length drawn on the image. Readers need it to judge size; a magnification number is useless once an image has been resized.
  • In Fiji: Analyze → Tools → Scale Bar. It only works if the pixel size is known (see section 2). Check Image → Properties first.
  • Use a round length (10 µm, 100 µm, 1 mm) and put it in a corner where it hides nothing. White on dark images, black on bright ones.
  • Add the bar to the crop as well as the overview; the two have different scales.
7 · Data

Files, metadata and backup

The microscope saves more than pictures. It saves the pixel size, the z-step, the objective, the laser powers and the time of every frame. This is the metadata, and analysis depends on it.

Keep the native file

  • Save in the instrument's own format: .lif on the Leica systems, .h5 on the Luxendo light sheet, the reconstruction output on the microCT. These keep all metadata.
  • If you need another format, export to TIFF (or OME-TIFF). It keeps the full brightness range and most metadata. The unit's h5 to TIFF converter and LIF scripts do this.
  • Never analyse a JPEG. JPEG throws away the brightness range (16-bit to 8-bit), compresses by discarding fine detail, and drops the pixel size. It is fine for a slide show, nothing else. PNG keeps the pixels but still loses the metadata.
  • Open everything in Fiji with Plugins → Bio-Formats (or just drag the file in). It reads all of these formats and shows the metadata under Image → Show Info.

Where the data goes

  • Instrument computers are not storage. Move your data to the image analysis server, then to your own lab storage.
  • Data on the analysis server is kept for no longer than 60 days, and may be removed sooner. Your lab folder on the server follows the same rule. Back up elsewhere (lab PC, Google Drive) as soon as the session ends.
  • Name files so that the metadata is in the name too: 2025-03-14_sampleA_DAPI-GFP_40x_z-stack_01.lif beats Image1.lif.
  • The image data management guide explains when to use remote desktop and when to map a network folder, and the server access manual has the connection steps.
Reference

Glossary

Objective
The lens closest to the sample. Its magnification and NA are engraved on the barrel.
NA (numerical aperture)
How wide a cone of light the objective collects. Higher is sharper. Cannot exceed the refractive index of the immersion medium.
Resolution
The smallest distance between two points that still appear as two. Roughly wavelength ÷ (2 × NA).
Pixel / voxel
One square of a 2D image / one box of a 3D image. Its physical size is the calibration.
Nyquist sampling
The rule that pixels must be at most half the size of the smallest detail you want to keep.
Histogram
Bar chart of how many pixels have each brightness value. The quickest way to spot saturation.
Saturation
Pixels clipped at the maximum value. Their true brightness is lost.
Bit depth
How many brightness levels a pixel can hold: 256 for 8-bit, 65 536 for 16-bit. Keep 16-bit for analysis.
Channel
One dye's image: a specific excitation colour plus a detector window.
Bleed-through
Signal from one dye appearing in another dye's channel. Checked with single-stain controls.
Photobleaching
Fading of fluorescence under illumination. Minimise light before the real acquisition.
Autofluorescence
Light a sample emits without any added dye. Strong in plant tissue.
Z-stack
A series of images at different focus depths, forming a 3D volume.
LUT (lookup table)
The colour map used to display brightness values. Changing it changes the display, never the data.
ROI
Region of interest: a selected area of an image, used for crops and measurements.
Metadata
The settings stored with an image: pixel size, z-step, objective, exposure, time stamps.
Bio-Formats
The Fiji plugin that opens microscope file formats and reads their metadata.
Bookit
The booking system for the unit's instruments. Sessions must be approved before use.

Still unsure about something? Ask the staff. A ten-minute conversation before a session saves hours of unusable data.
